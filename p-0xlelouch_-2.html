How to master distributed systems in 2026

1. Get a solid understanding of how a single machine works first (CPU, memory, disk, network). Distributed systems amplify single-machine problems, they don’t hide them.

2. Build and deploy a simple monolith before touching microservices. If you can’t reason about one process, you won’t reason well about ten.

3. Constantly ask “how does this work under the hood?” Curiosity is the real prerequisite for distributed systems.

4. Learn the core theory from first principles: processes, threads, clocks, time, ordering, and failure modes. These concepts show up everywhere.

5. Study the fundamentals: replication, sharding, leader election, and consensus (Raft, Paxos). Don’t just memorize them—understand what breaks when they fail.

6. Build a small multi-node system and deliberately kill nodes, drop packets, and introduce latency. Failure is the best teacher here.

7. Learn messaging systems deeply (Kafka, NATS, SQS) and understand backpressure, consumer lag, and at-least-once vs exactly-once semantics.

8. Understand CAP, but more importantly learn when it actually matters in real systems and when it doesn’t.

9. Go deep on a few data stores: Postgres for correctness, Redis for speed, and one distributed database (Cassandra/Dynamo-style) for scale.

10. Learn caching patterns and cache invalidation, and internalize why “cache is easy” is a lie.

11. Understand retries, timeouts, and idempotency together. Most outages come from getting this trio wrong.

12. Learn load balancing, health checks, and graceful degradation. A system that fails politely is a good system.

13. Read real production incident postmortems and ask yourself which design trade-off caused the failure.

14. Practice reasoning about latency, tail latency, and failure domains instead of average-case behavior.

15. Learn observability early: metrics, logs, traces, dashboards, and alerts are part of the system, not an afterthought.

16. Understand system evolution: schema migrations, rolling deployments, backward compatibility, and versioned APIs.

17. Read classic papers (MapReduce, Dynamo, Spanner, Bigtable, Kafka, LFS) to see why modern systems look the way they do.

18. Read real code when possible (etcd, Redis, Postgres internals) to connect theory with implementation.

19. Ask senior engineers or LLMs hard “what happens if this fails?” questions until your answers become instinctive.

20. Teach what you learn by writing or building small demos. If you can explain it clearly, you understand it.

Good luck 🤞

As a software engineer, your biggest asset isn’t just your salary - it’s your earning power over time. Managing risk isn’t optional if you want that to compound without blowing up. Here’s how I think about investing and risk management, in very practical terms:

1. Don’t treat your job as a “risk-free asset”
One company, one team, one tech stack is concentration risk. Skills get outdated, teams get reorged, companies downsize. Your first hedge is continuous upskilling and staying employable across companies, not just inside one.

2. Build an all-weather financial base first
Emergency fund (6–12 months), health insurance, and term insurance come before investing. This protects you from being forced to sell assets at the worst possible time.

3. Diversify beyond equity compensation
RSUs and ESOPs already tie your income to your company. Avoid doubling down by putting all your investments into the same sector or geography. If your job is tech-heavy, balance it with non-tech exposure.

4. Invest across asset classes
Equities for growth, debt for stability, gold as a hedge, and cash for flexibility. You don’t need to maximize returns everywhere - you need to survive every market cycle.

5. Geographic diversification is underrated
If you earn in INR, owning USD assets is risk management, not “foreign investing.” Currency depreciation is silent but brutal over decades. Global ETFs and US stocks are a long-term hedge.

6. Position sizing matters more than picking winners
A great idea with bad sizing can wipe you out. No single stock, crypto, or “high-conviction” bet should be able to ruin your sleep or your future.

7. Use hedges when risk is asymmetric
You don’t hedge all the time - you hedge when downside risk is large and known. Simple put options, asset allocation shifts, or cash buffers can save years of progress.

8. Avoid lifestyle inflation disguised as success
Bigger house loans, expensive cars, and fixed monthly obligations reduce your risk-taking ability. Flexibility is an asset. Renting longer can sometimes be the smarter risk decision.

9. Treat side projects as asymmetric bets
Small capital, high learning, high upside. Open source, startups, content, consulting - these don’t replace investing, they diversify you.

10. Optimize for staying in the game
The goal isn’t to get rich quickly. It’s to avoid permanent damage - financially and mentally - so compounding can do its work over 20–30 years.

If you want to be a top-tier backend engineer in 2026, here’s the path I’d recommend - in roughly the order I’d take it:

1. Data Structures & Algorithms (properly)
Not for LeetCode tricks, but to build real intuition: caches, queues, heaps, indexes, bloom filters, rate limiters. You should be able to implement the basics without panic.

2. Operating Systems fundamentals
Processes vs threads, memory management, virtual memory, syscalls, file descriptors, scheduling. Backend performance issues are often OS issues in disguise.

3. Networking & Distributed Systems basics
TCP vs UDP, HTTP/1.1 vs HTTP/2 vs HTTP/3, DNS, load balancing, retries, timeouts, backpressure. If you don’t understand networks, you don’t understand backends.

4. Databases (deep, not wide)
Pick one SQL database (Postgres) and go deep:
indexes, query planners, MVCC, isolation levels, WAL, replication.
Then learn why NoSQL exists, not just when to use it.

5. Consistency, Transactions & Failure Models
CAP, consensus (Raft/Paxos at a conceptual level), idempotency, exactly-once vs at-least-once, saga patterns. This is where many “senior” engineers fall apart.

6. A systems language (Go or Rust)

Go: concurrency, networking, cloud-native systems

Rust: safety, performance, correctness
Most modern infra and backend platforms are written in one of these.

7. Concurrency & Parallelism (in practice)
Thread pools, async runtimes, event loops, actor models, work queues.
Learn how things break under load, not just how they work on paper.

8. Observability & Production Debugging
Metrics, logs, traces, SLOs, alerting, profiling.
Learn to debug memory leaks, latency spikes, and cascading failures at 3am.

9. Cloud & Infrastructure fundamentals
Containers, Kubernetes basics, autoscaling, service discovery, CI/CD.
You don’t need to be a DevOps expert, but you must understand the platform your code runs on.

10. Security fundamentals
AuthN vs AuthZ, TLS, secrets management, common attack vectors, threat modeling. Backend engineers are security engineers whether they like it or not.

The pattern here is simple:
Easy backend jobs are over. CRUD alone won’t cut it.

Strong fundamentals let you move fast, reason under pressure, and design systems that survive reality - not just pass interviews.

There are no shortcuts anymore.
But if you build these skills seriously, even if you stumble, you’ll come out dangerously competent.

If you want to future-proof your career as a distributed systems programmer, stop chasing frameworks and start mastering the primitives that every large system is built on.

These are the things that will still matter 30–50 years from now, regardless of language, cloud, or hype cycle:

1. Concurrency & Parallelism
Threads, async, event loops, actors, scheduling.
Understanding how work actually runs on CPUs is non-negotiable.

2. Networking Fundamentals
TCP vs UDP, congestion control, TLS, HTTP/2 & HTTP/3, gRPC.
Distributed systems are just computers failing to talk to each other.

3. Failure Models
Timeouts, retries, partial failures, cascading failures, split-brain.
Real systems fail by default — resilience is the product.

4. Consistency & Coordination
CAP theorem (beyond slogans), quorum systems, leases, fencing tokens.
When state is shared, correctness becomes a design problem.

5. Distributed Storage Internals
Logs, LSM trees, B-trees, replication, sharding, compaction.
Data durability and performance shape everything upstream.

6. Time & Ordering
Logical clocks, vector clocks, monotonic vs wall time, clock drift.
If you don’t model time correctly, your system will lie to you.

7. Messaging & Streaming
At-least-once vs exactly-once, backpressure, consumer lag, replay.
Data in motion is harder than data at rest.

8. Load, Backpressure & Flow Control
Rate limiting, queues, bounded buffers, admission control.
Good systems degrade gracefully instead of falling over.

9. Observability as a First-Class Concept
Tracing, metrics, logs, SLOs, error budgets.
If you can’t see the system, you can’t operate it.

10. Security as a Distributed Problem
Identity, auth, key rotation, zero trust, blast-radius reduction.
Security failures scale just as fast as data does.

11. Operational Simplicity
Boring architectures, predictable failure modes, easy rollback.
Systems survive decades because humans can operate them.

12. Resource Economics
Latency vs throughput, memory vs CPU, network vs compute.
Every design decision is a cost trade-off, whether you admit it or not.

These aren’t trends.
They are physical laws of computation at scale.

Languages change. Clouds change. Tools come and go.
But distributed systems will always be about coordination, failure, and trade-offs.

Pick one of these areas.
Go uncomfortably deep.
Build systems that break and then learn why.

Do that, and you won’t worry about being replaced by AI.
You’ll be the person AI systems depend on.

I guarantee a huge chunk of Go developers can’t answer this cleanly.

And it shows up in production as:
• memory slowly creeping up
• goroutines that never die
• random latency spikes
• “it works locally” incidents

Let’s fix the mental model.

---

1. The question that breaks people

“Where does Go state live?”

Common answers:

“Inside the goroutine”

“On the stack”

“In Go runtime memory”

“Somewhere managed by Go”

All vague. None precise.

---

2. The correct answer (simple, but important)

Go state is just memory managed by the Go runtime.

When you write:

x := make([]int, 0, 1000)
go handleRequest(req)

You are allocating memory that lives either:

on the stack, or
on the heap

The compiler + runtime decide where.
The garbage collector decides when it can be freed.
---

3. The mental model most people miss

Goroutines do not own memory.
References do.

A goroutine exiting does not free memory.

If anything still holds a reference:

- a global variable
- a channel
- a closure
- a map
- a mutex-protected struct
- an uncanceled context

the memory stays alive.

This is the root cause of most “Go memory leaks”.

---

4. What actually lives in memory in a Go service

1. Heap

- structs, slices, maps
- data returned from functions
- captured variables in closures
- long-lived objects behind interfaces

2. Stacks

- every goroutine has a stack
- starts small, grows dynamically
- cheap and fast
- disappears when the function returns

3. Runtime structures

- goroutine metadata
- scheduler queues
- timers
- channels
- mutex state

Stacks are cheap.
Heap allocations are where you pay.

---

5. Why goroutines hurt production systems

Spawning goroutines is cheap.
Leaking them is expensive.

Classic bug:

go func() {
for {
work()
}
}()

That goroutine:

- never exits
- holds references forever
- prevents GC from reclaiming memory

Now do this per request.

You didn’t leak memory.
You designed an immortal workload.

---

6. Channels are memory anchors

Channels don’t just move data.
They retain references.

If a goroutine is blocked on:

ch <- bigStruct

Then:

- bigStruct stays in memory
- GC cannot collect it
- pressure builds silently

Unbounded or undrained channels = slow death.

---

7. Mutexes don’t leak memory — patterns do

This is fine:

sync.Mutex

This is dangerous:

map[string]*HugeObject

If you:

- never evict
- never delete
- keep pointers forever

Go is doing exactly what you asked.

It’s not a leak.
It’s unbounded retention.

---

8. Contexts: the #1 goroutine leak

Common mistake:

ctx := context.Background()
go doWork(ctx)

That goroutine has no stop signal.

Correct pattern:

ctx, cancel := context.WithCancel(parent)
defer cancel()

Contexts define lifecycle boundaries.

No cancel → no cleanup.

---

9. How to debug this properly

1. pprof (non-negotiable)

import _ "net/http/pprof"

Check:

- heap growth
- allocation hotspots
- goroutine count over time

2. Goroutine dumps

curl /debug/pprof/goroutine?debug=2

If goroutines only increase → you have a leak.

3. Ask the ownership question

Who owns this memory?
When should it die?
What reference keeps it alive?

---

10. The leaks nobody admits to

- fire-and-forget goroutines
- channels without consumers
- contexts never canceled
- maps used as caches with no eviction
- closures capturing large objects
- background workers started per request

These are not Go bugs.
They are lifecycle bugs.

---

11. The real lesson

Go doesn’t hide memory.
It exposes responsibility.

- Goroutines don’t clean up automatically
- Channels don’t drain themselves
- Contexts don’t cancel magically

GC doesn’t fix bad ownership

Go rewards engineers who think in lifetimes, ownership, and backpressure.

---

12. You don’t need to be a runtime expert

You just need to:

1. design goroutine lifecycles
2. cancel contexts
3. bound queues and channels
4. evict caches
5. look at pprof occasionally

That fixes most production Go problems.

We just fought a nasty Kafka bug in production and it all came down to consumer lag + silent connection drops.

Here’s what happened, what we learned, and what you should absolutely check in your own systems.

The Incident

We had a Kafka consumer that normally processes messages in microbursts.

Some partitions often sit idle for long stretches - minutes at a time because upstream traffic is uneven.

Everything was working fine… until suddenly it wasn’t.

Out of nowhere, one of the consumers started falling behind.
Lag kept increasing, even though traffic was low.
Logs showed nothing except a cryptic:

Connection reset by broker
Connection reset by peer
EOFException on FetchSessionHandler

And then… silence.

The process didn’t crash.
The consumer didn’t reconnect.
It just sat there alive-but-dead, holding a stale TCP connection and never issuing a new fetch request.

Classic zombie consumer.

The Root Cause

Kafka brokers drop idle connections after some timeout, especially when:

connections.max.idle.ms kicks in

The consumer hasn’t issued any fetch requests

The connection sits idle due to lack of traffic, long GC pauses, or slow processing

Network gear silently kills idle TCP sessions

When the broker finally closes the socket, the client sees a Connection reset.

But here’s the catch:

Your consumer may NOT automatically reconnect.

Depending on your client library and fetch loop implementation, you may accidentally:

- swallow the error

- exit the polling loop

- or keep retrying in a busy-wait without re-establishing a fresh connection

That’s exactly what happened to us.

The Fix: Explicit Reconnection Logic

We patched our consumer with a defensive pattern:

1. If the poll loop throws a transport-level exception (connection reset, EOF, broker disconnect):
→ immediately close the consumer and recreate a new one.

2. Add metrics around “time since last successful poll”
→ if this crosses a threshold, treat it as a stuck consumer and restart.

3. Add jittered backoff retries
→ to avoid hammering the broker during reconnection storms.

4. Ensure processing logic cannot block poll too long
→ delegate heavy logic to worker pools.

Now, instead of dying quietly, the consumer tears down its stale socket and becomes healthy again within seconds.

Some common Kafka Pitfalls That Bite Even Senior Teams

1. Low-traffic partitions are riskier than high-traffic ones

Idle partitions hit connection timeouts more often.

2. Kafka consumer loops must never block

If your processing takes 2 minutes, but max.poll.interval.ms is 1 minute → Kafka will kick you out.

3. Treat all I/O exceptions as signals to rebuild the consumer

Kafka clients aren’t always robust against partially-dead TCP sessions.

4. Monitor consumer-liveness, not just consumer-lag

Lag only increases when messages exist.
Stuck consumers on empty partitions show zero lag — and still be dead.

5. Networking gear can kill idle TCP connections silently

Load balancers, firewalls, NAT devices — any of them can drop your connection after X minutes of inactivity.

Patterns Every Kafka Team Should Implement

- Recreate consumers aggressively on network errors

- Health metrics: last poll time, last commit time

- Graceful backoffs with jitter to avoid herd effects

- Dedicated thread for the poll loop only

- Async processing (no blocking inside poll)

- Use session.timeout.ms and max.poll.interval.ms tuned realistically

- Watch for rebalance storms when consumers restart too often

Many Kafka outages don’t come from message volume they come from assumptions that “the client will reconnect automatically.”

In reality, a single half-dead connection can hold your entire pipeline hostage.

We learned this the hard way, but the fix now makes our system significantly more resilient.

If you run Kafka in production, don’t wait for a 3am pager.
Add reconnection logic and liveness metrics today.

> defined `.proto` files as the source of truth. shared them across repos. generated Go code with `protoc-gen-go`.

> swapped `encoding/json` for `proto.Marshal` and `proto.Unmarshal`. mostly mechanical. the compiler caught type mismatches instantly.

> switched inter-service calls to gRPC. HTTP/2, streaming, built-in deadlines. felt like upgrading from bicycle to bullet train.

> payload sizes dropped 40–60%. binary encoding, no field names, varint integers. less bytes over the wire, faster network hops.

> CPU usage on serialization dropped ~30%. Go's protobuf library is tight. no reflection, no runtime parsing.

> p99 latency between services fell by 25%. not magic - just less work per request compounding across hops.

> schema mismatches became compile errors. no more "why is this field null in prod" at 2am.

> debugging is harder. can't just `curl` and read the response. need tooling to decode.

> `.proto` management adds overhead. versioning matters. breaking changes need coordination.

Worth it though. JSON for external APIs. Protobuf for everything internal.

Kafka's "Zero-Copy" & Caching Magic

Kafka's efficiency comes from stepping aside and letting the Linux kernel handle data movement and caching directly.

1. Zero-Copy Data Transfer (sendfile())
Kafka uses the sendfile() system call to transfer data from disk to the network socket. This bypasses the application's userspace entirely, avoiding unnecessary data copying and CPU cycles. Data flows from the disk, through the kernel's page cache, and directly to the network interface card (NIC) buffer.

2. Memory-Mapped Files & Page Cache (mmap())
Kafka uses mmap() to map its index files directly into memory. This allows it to access file data as if it were in memory, with the OS managing loading and eviction. Both reads and writes go through the Linux page cache, leveraging decades of kernel optimization for caching without Kafka having to build its own layer.

Go's runtime is designed to efficiently manage thousands of concurrent operations by building on top of powerful Linux primitives.

3. Event-Driven Concurrency (epoll)
The Go netpoller uses epoll to manage network I/O. Instead of blocking a thread for each connection, a single event loop monitors many sockets. Goroutines are "parked" (suspended) until their socket is ready for I/O, at which point they are "unparked" and resumed. This allows for massive concurrency without the overhead of many OS threads.

4. Low-Level System Interaction (futex, madvise)
Go's high-level constructs map directly to efficient low-level syscalls. For example, Go's mutexes and other synchronization primitives are built on top of futex (fast userspace mutex) for efficient locking. The garbage collector uses madvise to hint to the kernel about unused memory pages, allowing the OS to reclaim them.

The syntax may be high-level, but the machinery underneath is pure Linux.

Languages and frameworks change, but these fundamental syscalls remain the bedrock of high-performance systems.

Love this problem, had to deal with similar thing for merging multiple market data feeds + app logs in Go.

Your semaphore idea is basically:
“let every stream push ahead a bit, but not too far”
which is exactly the right mental model.

There is a slightly simpler way though, if you’re ok with 1 in-flight item per stream:

Core idea:
for sorted streams, the globally smallest item is always among the current heads of each stream.
So you never really need more than 1 buffered element per input to decide the next output.

That means:

keep a min-heap of size ≤ N, one element per stream

only read from stream i when its previous element has already been popped from the heap

use Go’s “disable select case by setting channel to nil” trick instead of semaphores

Pseudo-architecture:

Have a coordinator goroutine:

tracks inHeap[i] bool for each input stream

tracks a min-heap of item{ value, streamIndex }

In a loop:

build a select over all input channels

for any i where inHeap[i] == true, set that channel to nil so select will never read from it

when select receives from ch[i]:

push (value, i) into the heap

set inHeap[i] = true

in the same loop, whenever the heap is non-empty:

pop the minimum item

send it to out

set inHeap[item.streamIndex] = false (now we’re allowed to read the next element from that stream)

Backpressure here is “free”:

producer writing to ch[i] will block if:

its previous item is still in the heap

and the consumer hasn’t popped it yet

we never let a single stream have >1 pending item in the heap

no extra semaphores, max memory ~O(N), and queue cannot be dominated by one fast input

Why this works well in practice:

sorted per-stream logs + 1 in-flight per stream is enough to always pick the global minimum

you avoid the “fast stream flooding” problem because that stream literally cannot progress until its head has been flushed to output

fairness comes from the heap: even if one channel is always ready, you still only ever keep one of its elements in play

Your approach (bounded semaphore, releasing on emit instead of receive) is basically a generalized version of this where maxBufferedPerChan > 1.
For pure chronological merge, the 1 item per stream version is often simpler to reason about and implement.

So if I had to summarise:

If you need strict merge of sorted logs → at most 1 pending item per stream + heap + nil-channel trick = simple and safe.

If you need more async slack per stream (burst tolerance etc.) → your bounded semaphore design is solid and more flexible.

Honestly, the hardest part here is not the heap, it’s understanding that “fairness” and “backpressure” can be done just by controlling how many in-flight items you allow per stream, not by trying to consume everything as fast as possible.

2. Backend sends this key to the client

3. Client passes the same key to the payment provider

4. Payment provider uses the key to dedupe retries

5. Webhook arrives → backend verifies using the same key

7. No matter how many retries occur, only one payment is ever processed

A Bf-Tree (Buffered-B-Tree) is like a classic B-Tree…
…except every internal node contains a write buffer.
Instead of writing small deltas directly to leaf pages, you:

- append updates to the internal node’s buffer

- flush them lazily down the tree

- group writes into huge sequential I/O batches

- avoid random writes entirely

It is orders of magnitude faster write throughput than B-Trees because of better read performance than LSMs for point lookups

- you need to process massive batches of updates
- you can parallelize your transformations
- you want fault tolerance & horizontal scaling
- you want to pre-aggregate results before storage

Map → split + transform
Reduce → aggregate + compress
Output → high-quality, sorted, ready-to-ingest chunks

OLTP-like point lookups

LSM-like write throughput

Everyone knows B-Trees. Very few people actually know Bf-Trees and LSM Trees properly.

Quick 101 for context.

B-TREE
This is the classic index used by MySQL / Postgres.
Balanced tree, each node is a page on disk, keys kept sorted.
Reads are great: O(log N) lookups with a small number of page reads.
Writes are ok but not amazing – every insert/update eventually touches multiple pages and causes random I/O.
Disk page size and in-memory page size are basically the same mental model.

BF-TREE (a.k.a. Bε-tree style)
Bf-trees try to fix the write problem without throwing away the B-tree idea.
Key trick: decouple on-disk page size from in-memory cache entry size and add buffers to internal nodes.
Instead of pushing every insert down to the leaf immediately, you buffer a bunch of updates in upper nodes and flush them in batches.
Result:
– Fewer random writes (you turn many small writes into fewer big ones)
– Much better write throughput, especially for large indexes
– Still pretty good point/range read performance, but more logic to walk buffered updates

It’s like saying: “I want a tree only, but I want it to behave more like a write-optimized structure.”

LSM-TREE
Log-Structured Merge Trees go even more extreme on write optimization.
– All writes are first appended to a log (memtable) in memory
– Flushed to disk as sorted runs (SSTables)
– Background compaction merges these runs to keep things somewhat tidy
Reads:
– Need to check multiple levels / files (use Bloom filters, indexes to speed this up)
– Great for write-heavy workloads and SSDs
– This is what RocksDB / LevelDB / Cassandra style systems use

Very rough intuition:
– B-TREE: classic, great for mixed read/write, simple mental model
– BF-TREE: B-tree DNA + smarter buffering = better writes, still decent reads
– LSM-TREE: fully commit to log + merge world, amazing writes, more complex read/compaction behavior

“Bf-trees decouple on-disk page size from cache entry size”, 💯
It basically means:
we stopped treating “what’s on disk” and “what sits hot in memory” as the same structure, and that gives us more freedom to batch, buffer and optimize writes.

Even I am Curious when/if databases like MySQL/Postgres will ship a modern write-optimized index next to their classic B-trees. The hardware world has changed a lot, but most mainstream indexes are still stuck in 1980s design space.

This lets you: drop old data, move cold data to cheaper nodes, scale only hot data.

For static data, still split by logical boundaries (region / tenant / type) if needed.

3. Shard design (most noobs mess up here)

Too many shards will kill your cluster faster than too much data.

Target 30–50 GB per shard as a rough rule.

1TB data → around 20–30 primary shards total across all indices is usually fine.

Start simple: maybe 3–5 primary shards per major index, 1 replica.

Watch actual shard sizes and adjust for future indices, not for already created ones.

4. Mapping (this decides performance + storage)

Explicit mappings only, do NOT rely on dynamic mapping for everything.

Use:

keyword for exact matches / aggregations

text only for real full text search fields

Turn off things you don’t need: _source only if you really can, doc_values or fielddata only on fields you aggregate/sort on.

Avoid indexing large blobs / descriptions unless you really search them.

5. Hardware / cluster

Prefer SSD, spinning disks gonna hurt you badly at this scale.

Start with 3 data nodes (for HA) if you can:

16–32GB RAM per node

Heap around 50% of RAM but never more than ~30–31GB

Use dedicated master nodes in bigger setups, but for small cluster you can share.

6. Indexing strategy for that initial 1TB load

Use the Bulk API only.

Aim for 5–20 MB per bulk request, tune based on response times.

Multiple parallel bulk workers instead of one gigantic request.

While bulk loading:

Set index.refresh_interval = -1 (disable refresh)

Optionally set number_of_replicas = 0 during load

After load finishes: set replicas back to 1 and refresh_interval to 1s or whatever.

After big import, you can run a forcemerge to reduce segments, but only on read-mostly indices, not on hot write ones.

7. Query design

Use filters (bool + filter) for conditions on keyword / numeric / date fields, cause filters are cacheable and much cheaper.

Limit from/size deep pagination, use search_after / scroll for large scans.

Monitor slow logs to see bad queries.

8. Observability

Watch: heap usage, GC, search latency, index latency, queue sizes, shard counts.

If nodes are dying, first check shards and mappings before buying more hardware.

you can design a very solid layout.
But the high level answer:
1TB / 16M rows is not crazy for Elasticsearch
You just need: good mapping, sane shard count, bulk indexing, and time based indices instead of one giant “data” index.
