solved Google Senior Software Engineer Interview question

implemented a movement sequence to escape a maze from an unknown starting position

built a solver that treats the maze as a grid with walls, open spaces, and one exit, where the agent has no feedback on its current location and only knows when it reaches the exit

the actual trick is tracking a belief set, the set of all cells the agent could currently be on since we don't know the real start. each move (up/down/left/right) is applied to every cell in that set at once, and walls cause different cells to collapse onto the same cell over time. ran a BFS over these belief sets until it shrinks down to just one cell, then appended a normal shortest path from there to the exit

that gives one fixed command sequence that works no matter where the agent actually started

Work story for you lot! As always, this is a bit long, but I love working and writing about it, and I hope y'all would love reading about it too!

I am working on a client project, where among other things, I have to make an SBC based HMI endpoint. The endpoint shall run scripts that helps visualize the state of the rest of the system, shall provide compute space for the client's own control algorithms to run, and shall have interfaces for controlling and reading digital and analog I/O within the system.

The client's control algo reads and actuated these I/O interfaces. Now, the SBC does not have to control them, we have separate boards that connect to a central logger. The SBC also connects to the logger over a USB interface, and for controlling the I/O, it only has to send a message to the Logger.

In the future, there is plans to have IP camera streams on a local ethernet network, and have the SBC hooked into it and help stream these feeds to a remote endpoint on the internet, for live, remote monitoring purposes.

Oh, and we are planning to have about 100 such systems deployed in the future, and we gotta actively design things for that. So keep that in mind too!

This is the most interesting problem that we have to face in this project. The debate is between linux + DE (desktop enviromnent) and android + kiosk mode customization. The decision boils down to 2 things - reproducibility of the image and all custom scripts that are being written for it, and ease of integration of said scripts that shall be written for it.

The advantage that I see at the moment is with Linux and a DE, and that boils down to the YOCTO build that we can have for it.

YOCTO, is a build system that helps us create custom OS distros, all tailor made by us for the application that we have intended. Via bitbake recipes, we can integrate as many scripts as we want, at any depth and degree of closeness to the linux kernel as we want, and ensure that the output binary that is flashed to the processor is consistent, across one or 100 such SBCs.

Android, in kiosk mode, is what I have seen being used MULTIPLE times in such SBC based HMI systems. The integration of python scripts on this is questionable for me - simply because I have never done it or seen it being done.

However, the beauty and smoothness of the UI that I have seen on kiosk-android deployments is unmatched - albeit the compute-heavy nature of Android.

And to make this more complicated - we may very well see an entirely different compute unit being used in this system in the future. This means that the OS image should also be compatible on the newer SBC or compute mechanism that we see in the system.

After talking to Gemini - YOCTO build for linux + a DE is the best option, and it boils down to the fact that YOCTO is build for shit like this. The meta-layers is all that needs to be swapped out for a change of SBC, and the bitbake scripts need to be setup ONCE for it to be reproducible as many times as we want it to be.

Also, integrating custom python logic and the client's algorithm on this is much easier than Android, that apparently does NOT support native python on it.

So yeah - as much as I hate to say it, all roads do indeed lead back to YOCTO.

For those who have read so far, first of all - thank you for your time. Second - I'll keep y'all posted about any further devs on this for sure.

Cheers!

A compute system needs some mechanism to measure the passage of time. At the lowest level, this is often provided by a physical, hardware timer driven by a periodic clock source. The timer generates an interrupt at a fixed interval, and it's Interrupt Service Routine updates a counter variable maintained by the system. Each increment of this variable is referred to as a "tick".

These ticks provide a local notion of elapsed time for the system. The operating system or firmware can use them to schedule tasks, implement timeouts and delays, measure execution intervals, timestamp events, and coordinate periodic operations.

The tick counter becomes a local reference for the passage of time for the computing system. Every event occurring within that compute system can be related to this reference.

On a multicore system, this notion of time may need to be shared or reconciled across cores; when multiple independent machines must coordinate, the problem extends into clock synchronization and distributed timekeeping.

G1 Garbage Collector has been around since JDK 9. Now that it is becoming the default in all environments, rather than just server environments, it felt like a good time to revisit how it works. Here is a quick gist...

The whole design of G1 is built around one simple idea - treat the heap as many small regions instead of two big generations. Most collectors before G1, like Parallel and CMS, split the heap into one young space and one old space, and collect each as a monolithic block.

G1 instead carves the heap into 1MB to 32MB regions, and each region gets tagged as Eden, Survivor, Old, or Humongous. A region's role can change from one collection to the next. It continuously tracks how much live data versus garbage sits in every region through a background concurrent marking cycle.

When a pause happens, G1 does not touch the whole heap. It evacuates the regions with the highest garbage ratio first, and it stops once it has done "enough" work to stay inside the pause budget (default 200ms).

By the way, evacuation is all about copying live objects out of a region into a fresh one, then freeing the old region entirely. G1 compacts a little on every single pause, as a side effect of how it collects.

Interestingly, to evacuate a region, G1 needs to know who and what points into it from elsewhere in the heap. Instead of scanning everything, each region keeps a `remembered set`, updated incrementally. So evacuating one region only means checking that region's own bookkeeping, not the whole heap.

Garbage collectors are super fun to dig into :) Always found them interesting. Got a good nudge to read about it when I stumbled on the JDK 27 release notes.

Hope this helps.

If you want to learn Harness Engineering, don't spend weeks searching for random tutorials.

The field is still relatively new, which means there isn't one standard textbook or university course that teaches everything. The best way to learn it right now is to study how real agent systems handle context, tools, memory, state, verification, permissions, and long-running tasks.

Here are 10 free resources I would use to understand Harness Engineering from the ground up:

1) Learn Harness Engineering

Probably the best place to start if you want a structured learning path. It covers environment design, state management, verification, and control systems, and includes hands-on projects where you compare prompt-only agents with properly designed harnesses.

2) OpenAI's Harness Engineering resources

A useful resource for understanding how agent-first development changes software engineering. Study how architectural constraints, repository-level instructions, validation, and the agent loop work together instead of treating the model as an isolated component.

3) Anthropic: Effective Harnesses for Long-Running Agents

Long-running agents introduce problems that don't appear in a simple chat interaction. Context gets lost, tasks span multiple sessions, and agents need ways to initialize, verify progress, and leave useful state behind for the next iteration. This is essential reading for understanding why the harness matters.

4) Anthropic: Demystifying Evals for AI Agents

A good harness needs a way to tell whether the agent actually succeeded. This resource helps explain outcome-based evaluation, trajectory evaluation, and why evaluating an agent is more complicated than checking whether its final answer looks good.

5) LangChain Deep Agents

One of the best ways to understand a harness is to inspect one. Deep Agents is an open-source agent harness that includes subagents, filesystem access, context management, persistent memory, human approval, tools, and more. Read the architecture and experiment with modifying individual pieces.

6) Awesome Agent Harness

This is a curated map of the entire ecosystem. It covers context engineering, tool design, MCP, agent loops, verification, memory, permissions, sandboxing, orchestration, evals, and research papers. Use this when you want to go deeper into a specific part of the stack.

7) Rasa's "Why Agents Fail"

A free, self-paced course focused on the practical question that matters most in production: why capable agents still break. It is particularly useful for understanding reliability problems beyond prompting and model selection.

8) Awesome Harness Engineering

Another strong curated repository, but with a more practical emphasis on reference implementations, tools, eval frameworks, memory, MCP, permissions, observability, and orchestration. Good for finding actual projects to study after learning the fundamentals.

9) DeepAgents and other open-source coding agent implementations

Don't just read about harness engineering. Clone an agent, inspect how it manages context, look at how tools are exposed, trace what happens after a tool fails, and understand how the agent decides whether to continue or stop. Reading the harness code teaches you things that theory alone won't.

10) Build your own harness

This is where everything starts making sense.

Take a simple model and give it a goal. Then gradually add a tool layer, persistent state, a task loop, context management, verification, retries, permissions, and clear stopping conditions. At each step, observe what new failure mode appears.

That is really the best way to understand Harness Engineering.

The biggest misconception is that Harness Engineering is another form of prompt engineering.

It isn't.

Prompt engineering focuses on what you tell the model.

Context engineering focuses on what information the model sees.

Harness engineering is broader. It is about designing the entire environment in which the model operates: the tools it can use, the state it maintains, the feedback it receives, the actions it is allowed to take, and the mechanisms that determine whether it is actually making progress.

The model may be getting smarter every few months.

The question is whether the system around it is good enough to let that intelligence do useful work.

That's where Harness Engineering begins.

~~~

High performance → we're dealing with orders of magnitude more Actors than Nomad / Kubernetes containers, so our write throughput needed to scale linearly

Scale writes horizontally → we wanted a "just add nodes" solution to scaling throughput, instead of maintaining a single large primary VM (e.g. Postgres) or weakening consistency guarantees (e.g. Cassandra)

Strong consistency → eventual consistency creates an incredibly complicated class of bugs

Enable user-facing database → we provide a KV API (now SQLite) to Actors, our database needed to be able to support that

Self-hostable & permissive licensing → no proprietary components or vendor lock

Don't shard Postgres → you're basically rebuilding a slower, more complicated database when you do this

I got several DMs asking me to explain what I meant by DFS in my post.

DFS stands forDepth-First Searchwhich is a graph/tree traversal algorithm.But I also think of it as a mental model for learning.

The idea is that you pick a path and follow it to the end.

You don't have to stop every other hour to think, "Oh right, I need to learn 10 prerequisites before I can even understand this concept".You just follow along till you understand it or get stuck because of a critical missing understanding.

Then you backtrack, filling in the gaps till you can get unstuck, and proceed deeper into the rabbit hole.

There's another reason why I like this analogy though.

If you approach learning fundamentals on their own, without having first struggled with the harder concepts that those fundamentals are prerequisites for, you can feel underwhelmed.

You might feel like, "Oh, this is just a fundamental".

You see a data structure or an algorithm or a piece of OS theory or a DB primitive and think, "Well there's a neat little toy example where it comes up".

But if you had first grappled with the harder concept that required that fundamental, your view of the fundamental would be quite different.

You would see it in a larger context.

The fundamental wouldn't be a standalone object of appreciation, but rather a building block to something you were trying to understand.

You don't learn B+ trees because it's on your roadmap of things to learn about databases, but because you wanted to understand storage engines, and you need to know B+ trees to understand storage engines.

You don't learn WAL as just another log, but as a critical enabler of durability, recovery, and consistency in the face of failures, crashes, and aborts - and how that feeds into the larger system that you're trying to understand.

The harder concept is providing you with the context to understand the importance of a fundamental.

And since you've already struggled with the harder concept, you now want to go deeper into understanding the foundational piece.

You can look at the primitive in less of a vacuum, and more in terms of how it fits into a larger system, how it's used, what limitations it has, the tradeoffs it makes, etc.

That, to me, is the really interesting part about this type of learning.

You get to do that kind of analysis only when you first struggled with the harder concept, and then went back and filled in the gaps.

database internals one-liners

- you can't optimize for both writes and reads completely, you have to pick one
- try to minimize updating a structure as much as you can for faster writes
- try to minimize the number of such structures for faster reads
- you can't do both
- persistence is costly but important. you need to find a good time to batch flushes, too often or too infrequently can both hurt
- fsync isn't a magic guarantee. you need to understand what durability guarantees your storage stack actually provides
- encoding and decoding stuff should be optimized. you need to treat your file as your data structure
- optimizing around a fixed page size is important for caching and speed
- deletion is expensive. optimize your tombstone and vacuum strategy
- transactions are important. rollback and recovery strategies are even more important in distributed environments
